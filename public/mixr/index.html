Case study

Designing Mixr by building it

DJ software is powerful and intimidating.
Mixr is an iOS DJ app that turns remixing into something anyone who has edited a video already knows how to do: clips on a timeline, drag and drop, undo. I designed it and wrote it in SwiftUI, so every decision got tested in motion and sound instead of argued over a mockup.

01 — Problem

DJing shouldn’t require already feeling like a DJ.

I wanted to learn to DJ. No music theory, no instrument, never mixed a song — so instead of buying a controller I started building the app I wanted to learn on.

Every tool I opened was organised around a booth: EQ knobs, cue points, beat grids, and a vocabulary that assumes you are already performing. None of it is wrong. It is aimed at someone who already knows the craft.

Traditional DJ software

  • EQ knobs and gain staging
  • Cue points and beat grids
  • Routing controls
  • Beatmatch by ear, in real time
  • One take, performed live

Mixr

  • One timeline, many lanes
  • Clips you drag, split and delete
  • A playhead you scrub
  • Beat-based transition lengths
  • Composed over time, undoable

The question the whole project answers

What would DJ software look like if it were designed for someone who already knows how to edit a video, but has never touched a turntable?

02 — Insights

From live performance to visual composition.

A few weeks in I realised I wasn’t designing a DJ app. I was designing an audio editor — my design questions had quietly become music questions: where should one song end, how long should two overlap.

That reframing is the product. The interface isn’t the thing being made. The remix is.

The reference set was never DJ software. It was CapCut, Final Cut Pro, Premiere Pro, DaVinci Resolve, TikTok’s editor and GarageBand. Three of them left a fingerprint you can point at in the build.

Concretely: songs become coloured waveform lanes, audio becomes draggable clips, effects become cards, and the playhead is the anchor every action reads from.

The interface, today's build

Four songs imported. Left: the track list. Centre: the lanes and playhead. Right: solo, mute and volume. Bottom: effects. Every song gets its own color, and that color is reused by its waveform, its clip outline, its volume slider and its transition geometry — so the lane you are editing is never ambiguous.

03 — Decisions

What I left out is what made the rest possible.

No decks, no crossfader, no hot cues, no per-channel EQ. Every one is a real DJ tool, and every one asks the beginner to learn the booth before they can hear a result.

I built it in passes, each with a scope written down first — and, more usefully, a freeze list. Almost every task started with what not to touch, because the failure mode of moving fast is a fix that quietly redesigns three things you were happy with.

Not one glow value shared across six cards. Yellow reads brightest at any given number, then red, then pink, so each is pulled back until the row looks evenly lit. Auto gets pushed the other way on selection — it is the card I want people to press.

The same row in the app, selecting Echo. The glow spills onto the neighbouring cards rather than stopping at the card's own edge — which is the part that only ever looked right in motion.

Professional plugins expose parameters that mean something to an engineer and nothing to a beginner, so the set stays small, named and preset-backed — one intensity each.

The red card took longest to name. Warmth described a quality rather than an action. Haze was at least picturable. It ended up Flanger — the one place I let a technical term stand, because no everyday word for it isn’t a lie.

Why an audio effect is called Blur

I borrowed the name from visual editing so a beginner can predict what it will feel like before hearing it. Someone who has never heard the phrase “low-pass filter” has definitely blurred a photo, and the guess they make from the word is the right one.

04 — Behavior

The timeline should rearrange itself the way you expect.

Split, duplicate, delete, speed and drag are the whole editing language. Naming them was easy. Deciding how they behave took two days and was the hardest design work in the project.

Drop into a clip body

  1. 01 · RestingTwo clips, one lane.
  2. 02 · LiftedDetaches, scales 1.04, follows the cursor.
  3. 03 · Split previewedThe destination shows where it will cut.
  4. 04 · SandwichedDestination splits; the clip lands between both halves.

Drop a clip into the body of another and Mixr splits the destination and inserts between the halves, rather than shoving it aside. Video editors have done this for years — but they make you choose it first, with a modifier for insert and a plain drag for overwrite. Here there is no mode to pick.

Predictability comes from resolving the drop in a fixed order rather than from one clever rule. The pointer is tested against five cases in priority, and the first that matches wins.

  1. Ghost restoreReleased within a hair of where it started — put it back, ripple nothing.
  2. Edge snapInside a neighbour's 12–16 pt edge zone — snap flush before or after it.
  3. Literal gapLanded in empty space — leave it exactly where it was let go.
  4. Insert into bodyInside a clip, past its edge zones — split there and sandwich.
  5. Free placementAnything else — place it and let the lane reflow.

The preview is the contract: whatever the neighbours animate to while you are dragging is exactly what happens when you let go.

And the timeline is only honest if the sound follows it. A clip carries its own effect settings, and they have to take hold and let go at its boundaries. Splitting a clip splits its audio. Undo restores both, or the two drift apart and the app starts lying to you.

05 — What changed

Every one of these felt right until I ran it.

At one point the lift came out and the clip went back to sliding along the lane. Still functional, still “correct,” and immediately worse — nothing was ever picked up, it just slid. Getting the lift back was a whole task of its own.

The play button was centred. It didn't look centred.

For weeks the transport cluster felt slightly off. My instinct was to nudge the play button, which was wrong — it was already dead centre. The time, BPM and key sat to its right and dragged the perceived centre left.

The fix moved everything except the button: the cluster shifts 20–30 px right, time, BPM and key tighten into one metadata block, their labels shrink, and the play glow pulls back 10–15% because the bloom made it read larger than it is.

Chosen: optical balance over equal gaps. The navbar is spaced as five groups — brand, history, playback, metadata, export — with intentional distances between them rather than one evenly spaced row. Related things sit close; unrelated things get air.

Then

Now

Bass Boost is gone — it is a mix correction, not a creative choice, and it made every remix louder rather than more interesting. “Pitch Up” became “Pitch” once the control could go both ways.

Auto’s first version applied effect presets across a whole track. That is decorating, not arranging, and it produced remixes that sounded processed rather than edited. It was rebuilt to do what a person would do: split, trim, duplicate, move and overlap real clips on the real timeline.

The line that fixed Auto

Preservation is a constraint, not the objective. An Auto remix that only makes one safe change is a failure even though it broke nothing — and sound effects alone do not count as a transformation. It has to feel intentionally edited in several places, or it hasn't done the job.

The rule Auto is built against

Everything Auto creates has to be something you can see, edit and undo. It writes ordinary clips and ordinary transitions onto the same timeline you edit by hand — nothing it makes is a special object you are locked out of, and the whole run collapses into a single undo.

06 — Results

Built, not finished.

Working today

  • Import, with artwork and metadata
  • Waveform, BPM and key analysis
  • Split, duplicate, delete, speed
  • Drag, drop and sandwich insertion
  • Beat-based transitions with curves
  • Effects and sound-effect insertion
  • Solo, mute, per-track volume
  • Undo, redo and multiple projects
  • Responsive across iPhone, iPad and Mac

Still building

  • Full per-clip DSP on every effect
  • Export reliability at length
  • Playback performance under load
  • Testing with actual beginners
  • Onboarding for a first remix
  • TestFlight beta, once the developer account clears

The last two passes were the unglamorous ones: one layout that holds on iPhone, iPad and a resizable Mac window, and before it a full visual audit — a written report on cohesion, hierarchy and optical balance, with no code changed until I had read it. Auditing first is what stopped the responsive work quietly becoming a redesign.

Now I’m waiting on the Apple Developer Program so I can put a beta in people’s hands. That is the next real test — everything above is a designer judging a tool built for beginners, and beginners have not used it yet.
