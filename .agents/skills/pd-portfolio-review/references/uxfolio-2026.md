# UXfolio State of UX Portfolios 2026: checks and evidence

Source: https://uxfol.io/state-of-ux-portfolios-2026/report

Use these figures exactly. Cite them as "UXfolio 2026, ch. X.Y". Do not round, combine or invent numbers. If a point has no figure, say it is a judgement call.

**First 30 seconds** (ch. 1)
- 78% rely on the portfolio and case studies to judge fit, ahead of industry experience (46%) and CV (40%). (1.1)
- 65% spend under four minutes on a first look: 10% under a minute, 55% 1 to 4 minutes. (1.2)
- Quote: "When I have 100 portfolios to go through, I may have only 30 seconds for each one." (1.2)
- The scan is a gate. Shortlisted case studies get a closer read later. Check the portfolio works for both: easy to skim in minutes, and solid under a close read. (1.3)
- 71% say their organisation doesn't screen portfolios with AI. The reader is a person, so do not judge it on keywords. (1.4)
- About section: 53% rate it important or extremely important (35% + 18%). Career path and tone of voice matter; personal detail less so. (1.5)
- The story must match the CV and LinkedIn. Inconsistent roles or dates are early red flags. The report gives no percentage. (1.6)

**Format and access** (ch. 2)
- 72% prefer a website. Figma prototype 30%, PDF 27%, slides 26%, Behance or Dribbble 14%. (2.1)
- Quote: "If a portfolio is shared as a Google Drive folder, I tend to move it to the back of the pile." (2.1)
- What stops a review immediately: broken links 75%, missing password 61%, requires download 40%, distracting animation 35%. (2.2, 2.3)
- Quote: "Failing to check that a portfolio is accessible suggests that they may not double-check their work." (2.2)
- Templates: 76% spot one when the same design appears across candidates, 51% notice placeholder copy, 49% notice a platform subdomain. A template does not count against them on its own. Flag leftover placeholder text. Do not flag the template. (2.4)

**Finding the right project** (ch. 3)
- What decides which case study gets opened first: relevance 73%, clear outcome in the preview 40%, visual appeal 39%, title 33%, first listed 28%. (3.1)
- Most valued elements: relevant case studies 89%, clear navigation 83%, mobile optimised 45%, visually ambitious design 40%, testimonials 11%. (3.2)
- Number of projects: 71% prefer three or four. 10% want 1 to 2, 7% want 5 or more. (3.3)
- The first slot reads as their best work. The report shows 31% for this but does not state the question behind it. Present it as "about a third, per UXfolio's graphic". If you need a firm number, use the 28% "first listed" figure from 3.1. (3.4)
- Project cards: 27% flag cards without enough context. A card should say what the project is, who it was for and what kind of product it is. (3.5, Bonus #5)

**Inside a case study** (ch. 4)
- What makes one strong: explanation of decisions 64%, business impact 46%, scannable layout 45%, constraints 31%. Visuals only 20%. (4.1)
- Biggest weaknesses: no explanation of thinking 49%, overly long or dense 44%, not showing outcome 43%, feels exaggerated 38%, no problem statement 38%, unclear ownership 33%, artefacts without context 30%. (4.2)
- Outcomes: the problem is missing outcomes. Documenting the process is rarely the issue (14% name "documenting all the steps"). If the product has not launched, they should say how success will be measured. 4.3 repeats the 4.2 question, so cite them as one finding. (4.3)
- Ownership: 33% flag an unclear role. Look for a plain line saying what the designer did and what the team did. (4.4)
- Quote: "It's mostly an easy-to-catch lie that you have 'done it all' on your own. Nobody can." (Bonus 6.4)

**NDA work** (4.5)
- 84% expect NDA work to appear in some form; only 16% say leave it out. Preferred: brief description and discuss in interview 35%, anonymised visuals 31%, general terms without visuals 18%.
- Quote: "I'd rather see anonymised work from a real client than a concept redesign of a famous app with no real constraints."

**AI use and disclosure** (ch. 5)
- 100% of reviewers have noticed AI in a portfolio. (5.1)
- How they spot it: buzzwords without detail 68%, no personal voice 62%, visuals that don't match the process 41%, generic process with no artefacts 39%, generic headers 33%. (5.2)
- Low visual quality is a red flag for 55%; visual ambition matters to 40%. Both figures come from other charts (Bonus list and 3.2), so cite each once. Clean beats impressive. (5.3)
- Obvious AI overuse is a red flag for 43% (same Bonus figure). Not using AI at all can read as falling behind, especially for senior designers. (5.4)
- Good disclosure: name the tool and the task, show the prompt or first draft and what changed, separate their own decisions from the output, and don't claim to be an "AI expert". (5.4)

**The ranked red flags** (Bonus): check each one and mark it present, absent or unclear.
1. Very low overall visual quality, 55%
2. Hard to navigate or confusing structure, 48%
3. Obvious overuse of AI, 43%
4. Overly long or dense, 42%
5. Case study cards without enough context, 27%
6. Irrelevant case studies, 20%
7. Distracting animations, 20%
8. Slow loading or technical issues, 18%

Note: "overly long or dense" is 42% here but 44% in 4.2, and "distracting animation" is 20% here but 35% in 2.2. They come from different questions. Cite the one that matches your point. Do not mix them.

**Reviewer advice** (Bonus 6.1 to 6.5): use these to frame the fixes.
- The portfolio is a product and the hiring manager is the user.
- One relevant case study beats five generic ones.
- Show impact, but don't inflate it: "having nothing is better than putting something that is not real."
- Explain why you did something, as well as what you did.
- Keep it short and in your own voice.
